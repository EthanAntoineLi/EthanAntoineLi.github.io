The five-axis arm, from my EPQ:

01 / Why

Rescue, without
a second rescuer.

I ski, and every season I see people get hurt on the slopes — and more people go out onto the same ground to bring them back. Why not send a robot? I found very few, and a 3D printer meant I could make the parts for one. A whole robot was too much for one project, so I started with its arm.

Ski rescue · the full robot is out of scope · the arm first

02 / The brief

Eight requirements,
set before the build.

Five functional requirements say what the arm has to do; three non-functional ones limit how it gets made. Each one comes back at the end, scored.

03 / Open or closed loop

Trust the input,
or measure the output.

Before choosing a motor, the first question is whether anything checks its work. Each architecture is a trade-off: simplicity against self-correction.

The arm ends up closed-loop · see 04 / Motors

Open loop
input → controller → actuator → output
Runs on preset inputs and timings and never checks its output. Fewer parts, lower cost, easier to design and no measuring delay — but disturbances and calibration errors go uncorrected.
e.g. a toaster on a timer · fixed-time traffic lights
Closed loop
input → controller → actuator → output ↺ sensor
Sensors feed the output back so it is compared with the target, and the system corrects itself. More sensors, more cost and slower — but it rejects disturbances and needs no perfect calibration.
e.g. cruise control · an automatic water-level controller

04 / Motors

Steppers, geared
and closed-loop.

The arm is meant for a battery-powered robot, so only DC motors were in the running. Brushed motors wear out; brushless ones need a controller to commutate. A stepper moves in exact steps without a sensor, but not with enough torque alone — so each joint got a gearbox, and a driver that closes the loop over RS485.

UMIR57 ×1 · UMIR42 ×4 · stepper + gearbox + driver = servo

05 / Gearboxes

Worm at the shoulder,
planetary for the rest.

Spur and bevel suppliers wanted 15 or more units; I needed at most six. A worm drive self-locks, so it holds a load without drawing current, but sits at a right angle. A planetary is coaxial and compact, but back-drives, so holding still costs current and heat. UMot sold matched sets one at a time.

06 / Torque check

One joint
comes up short.

Holding still, each joint carries everything beyond it: mass × distance × g. With 500 g at the tip, the shoulder needs 6.24 N·m, or 12.5 N·m with a safety factor of 2. Its worm gearbox is rated 5.4 N·m. The full calculation was only finished after assembly, when the actuator could no longer be changed.

J2 · 9.81 × 0.6358 kg·m ≈ 6.24 N·m · FR-01 not met

07 / Design

Six axes,
drawn in Shapr3D.

Every part was modelled before it was printed: six axes on screen, five of them built. Shapr3D’s direct modelling took the least effort to learn, it fit the budget, and it runs on an iPad, so the modelling could carry on away from the desk.

6 axes drawn · 5 built · exported as STEP

Software
SolidWorks The industry standard, but a basic licence was $235 a month
AutoCAD Trialled; the workflow wasn’t intuitive and was hard to master
Shapr3D Direct modelling, free tutorials, iPad · $11 a month Chosen

08 / Printing

Printed in PLA,
held with brass.

Every link was printed in PLA on a Bambu Lab H2S: the cheapest filament, and the easiest to print. Overhangs stand on tree supports with a PETG interface that peels off cleanly. Printed threads stripped at the first assembly, so the final links take M3 screws in heat-set brass inserts.

09 / Revision

One link,
drawn twice.

A torque check while modelling caught what the sketches hadn’t. In Rev A the shoulder motor sat level with the base axis, so joint 1 carried every motor beyond it. Rev B angles the link to lift that motor 56 mm above the axis, where its weight works against the rest of the arm.

Link J1 → J2 · J1 now holds 4.37 N·m on a 19 N·m drive

10 / Control

Five drivers, one bus,
no reply.

RS485 is half duplex, and a microcontroller would have needed extra parts to know when to talk and when to listen. So a PC drives the arm: a USB-to-RS485 converter, Python’s MinimalModbus as the master, and the five drivers daisy-chained A to A, B to B, at addresses 1 to 5. Every request timed out.

PC master · USB → RS485 · Modbus-RTU · slaves 1–5

Fault log
Symptom No reply from any driver. A Modbus slave always answers a frame addressed to it, so silence means nothing was getting through.
Checked A and B swapped both ways; a common ground connected. Still nothing.
Likely cause MinimalModbus defaults to 8N1. The drivers expect odd parity, so every byte fails their check and every frame is discarded — found afterwards, reviewing the fault with an AI assistant (AI log).
Next limit A driver accepts at most 16 registers per request.
Lesson I tried fixes one after another. Checking first whether any valid bytes reached the drivers would have pointed to the settings, not the wiring.

11 / Cost

¥3,483.58,
74% over budget.

The budget was ¥2,000. The five gearmotors were about 70% of the spend, which is why the build stopped at five joints: a sixth would have added roughly ¥400. Cost shaped earlier calls too — no bulk-only gearboxes, UMot on price and quality, Shapr3D over SolidWorks.

12 / Evaluation

Four met, three missed,
one untested.

The arm doesn’t run yet, so it was judged by analysis, not by test. The torque was calculated, but the shoulder actuator was chosen by judgement. A calculation only helps a design if it is used to make the decision.

J2 · 0.84 N·m (13%) short before any safety factor

13 / Next

Make the bus talk,
then rebuild the shoulder.

The arm doesn’t meet its aim yet, but it is a clear starting point. The likely fault needs no new parts, only a few lines of code; the shoulder needs an actuator chosen from the numbers.

Then FR-05 can be tested · ±2 mm over 10 trials

  1. Match the serial settings. Set MinimalModbus to the drivers’ odd parity and check every other default against them.
  2. Trace one request. Joint 1 alone, one register, debug mode on, and a second USB-to-RS485 adapter listening on the wire. A reply clears the settings; an exception means a wrong register or address; a request with no reply, a wrong frame; an empty bus, the converter.
  3. Add drivers one at a time. Each with its own address, every driver read back before the next goes on, so a new fault points at the last one connected.
  4. Second iteration. A shoulder actuator chosen from the calculation, and lighter links to cut the torque from the arm’s own weight.
